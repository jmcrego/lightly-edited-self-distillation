# Training an SLM on Minimally Post Edited Translations to Limit Distribution Shift

## Why Fine Tuning on Reference Translations May Disrupt Existing Translation Behavior

Supervised fine-tuning (SFT) trains the model to predict every token of a reference translation. However, translation admits many valid formulations: an accurate and fluent translation produced by the model may differ in wording and syntax from an equally high-quality reference translation. SFT therefore shifts the model toward the references’ distribution, which may differ substantially from its existing translation preferences. When adaptation uses only a few tens of thousands of sentence pairs, the data may provide insufficient coverage for the model to generalize these new patterns reliably, while the resulting updates may still disrupt existing translation behavior. This can reduce fluency or other previously acquired abilities, potentially even within a single epoch. The concern is therefore not necessarily poor reference quality, but overly strong adaptation toward a different, narrowly sampled distribution. Training on minimally corrected model outputs seeks to limit unnecessary changes to already-effective behavior while teaching the desired improvements.

This project connects research on training-data distributions, domain adaptation, and the preservation of existing translation capabilities. Li et al. [1] link supervised training data to translationese and report improvements from polishing or filtering targets. Yang et al. [2] investigate self-generated fine-tuning data as a means of narrowing the distribution gap and mitigating forgetting on other tasks. These findings motivate using the model’s own translations as the starting point for adaptation. However, the effects of parallel fine-tuning are not uniform: Stap et al. [3] observe fewer literal translations after fine-tuning, alongside degradation in several other abilities. Li et al. [10] provides a direct precedent for correcting student translations and fine-tuning on the resulting outputs; our experiments extend this principle with an explicit minimum-edit requirement and an emphasis on retaining fluency during domain adaptation and feature learning rather than retaining the model ability on other downstream tasks.

Research on specialized translation supports both the objectives of this project and the need for careful evaluation. Rios [4] demonstrates benefits from medical instruction tuning with specialized terminology, while preference optimization using post-edits [5] improves terminology accuracy without significant losses in COMET. Conversely, Hu et al. [7] identify domain overfitting and catastrophic forgetting after domain-limited adaptation; Saunders and DeNeefe [8] link forgetting in neural machine translation to the target vocabulary coverage of adaptation data; and Liu and Niehues [9] identify the relative scale of the model and training data as an important determinant of forgetting. Scholz et al. [6] further show that preserving general capabilities does not necessarily preserve translation-specific instruction following. Together, these studies motivate testing whether minimally corrected model outputs can support domain competence and prompt-specified translation requirements while preserving existing fluency. They also justify evaluating domain accuracy, fluency, instruction following, and retained general translation quality separately.

## Experiments

This recipe aims to improve a translation model’s competence in one or more domains while preserving its existing fluency and other previously acquired capabilities. It starts with an SLM, such as a decoder-only LLM with roughly 9–12B parameters and uses a strong teacher to correct its in-domain translations with the fewest changes needed to ensure accurate interpretation of domain-specific concepts and appropriate use of terminology, register, and linguistic conventions, while preserving already-correct and fluent wording. The SLM is then fine-tuned on these corrected outputs. The approach seeks to teach domain-specific improvements while limiting unnecessary changes to the model’s existing translation behavior. In comparison, fine-tuning on reference bitext may introduce more extensive changes in wording and style, potentially contributing to catastrophic forgetting. More formally, let M₀ denote the original translation model and T the stronger teacher. A conventional reference pair is denoted by (Xᵣ, Yᵣ), where Xᵣ is the source sentence and Yᵣ its reference translation. In our recipe, Xᵈ denotes a source sentence from domain d, and Yᵈₘ₀ denotes its translation generated by M₀. The teacher receives (Xᵈ, Yᵈₘ₀), and any translation requirement, then produces Y′ᵈₜ by minimally correcting the model’s translation as previously indicated. Unlike training on reference targets Yᵣ, this approach uses targets derived from the model’s own translations (Xᵈ, Y′ᵈₜ), with the aim of acquiring domain competence while limiting unnecessary changes to its existing translation behavior.

A second experiment applies the same principle to learning prompt-specified requirements: user-dictionary terminology (UDs), formality, locale, appropriate use of translation-memory examples (TMs), and formatting through tags. M₀ first generates translations from prompts containing these requirements. The teacher then makes the minimum corrections needed to satisfy the applicable requirements while maintaining accuracy and fluency. M₀ is fine-tuned on the corrected outputs using the same feature-conditioned prompts, with the aim of improving its ability to follow these instructions while preserving its existing translation competence.

## Teacher Post Editing Contract

Provided the source (and translation reference if available), full conditioning context, and student translation:

- Instruct the teacher to correct mistranslations, omissions, additions, grammar, and clear unnaturalness by introducing **minimum editions** (subordinate to adequacy, and natural target language). Preserve already correct wording and syntax in student translation; return the student translation untouched if it meets all requirements.
- If a reference is available, show it as evidence to check meaning or domain conventions.
- Require a brief structured reason for each edit (only the corrected translation is used for the SFT target).

**The human reference guides the correction of domain-specific errors rather than serving as a wording template. The teacher uses it to identify relevant improvements while preserving the model’s already-correct phrasing. This seeks to limit unnecessary stylistic changes. The reference translation should supply selected domain guidance:**

- **terminology** and **multiword expressions** with their context-dependent senses, _[we call these UDs]_
- conventional **phraseology** and reusable **translation patterns** with explicit slots and applicability conditions. _[we call these TMs]_

A correct balance of samples regarding ratio of rewrites must be found, allowing for both: unchanged and largely edited examples (badly misunderstood source sentences may need a full rewrite).

Using English → French medical translation, the distinction is **which information to extract from the reference**, rather than copying its full sentence. The following categories capture domain-specific language that can be extracted from a reference translation to guide targeted corrections:

| Feature | Example | Guidance to extract |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Terminology** | The patient was **discharged** yesterday. → Le patient est **sorti de l’hôpital** hier. | Here, _discharge_ means leaving hospital; in _nasal discharge_, it means _écoulement nasal_. |
| **Multiword expression** | The patient developed **acute kidney injury**. → Le patient a développé une **insuffisance rénale aiguë**. | Treat the expression as a clinical concept, rather than translating _injury_ independently as _blessure_. |
| **Phraseology** | The treatment was **well tolerated**. → Le traitement a été **bien toléré**. | Retain the conventional expression for treatment tolerability, adapting agreement: “La procédure a été **bien tolérée**.” |
| **Translation patterns** | The patient tested positive for influenza. → Le test de dépistage de la grippe était positif. | Pattern: “tested positive for **[condition]**” → “le test de dépistage de **[condition]** était positif”, when describing a diagnostic test result. |

These categories overlap. The practical distinction is that **terminology identifies a concept, phraseology supplies a conventional expression, and a translation pattern describes how a construction can be adapted across examples**. Even then, the teacher should keep another accurate, conventional formulation already produced by M₀ unless a specific domain requirement calls for changing it.

## Experimental Framework

Start with SFT, LoRA is a practical controlled alternative to full fine-tuning. Tune learning rate, epochs, adapter capacity, and rewrite ratio on dataset. Standard SFT applies loss to every token of Y′, even if the teacher changed only a word. Edit weighted loss (lower on unchanged tokens), or preference training on Y′ versus Y are subsequent ablations. Post edit preference optimization for ambiguous terminology has supporting results [5], but does not validate this entire pipeline.
